# Mathathon Round 1: Judge Rules

In Round 1 you score the presentations of the teams assigned to you, together with their Q&A. Separately, a panel of seven AI models (the LLM panel) reviews each team's written report. Its scores are advisory. Scores are used only to build a shortlist, and the judges decide the winners.

You take three steps on the SAIR competition platform: registration (§1), problem selection (§2), and scoring (§3). The shortlist, triage, and deliberation are in §4. The participants' rules are in [Participant Rules](participant-rules.md). All times are Pacific Time.

## Key dates

| When | What you do |
|---|---|
| Before Nov 13 | Register and accept the judge agreement (§1). Choose the problems you can judge, and report any conflict of interest (§2). |
| Nov 15, 10:00 AM | Submission deadline. Your presentations are assigned (§2). |
| Nov 15, before 12:00 PM | Attend the judges' briefing (10–15 minutes). Check your assigned presentations on the platform. |
| Nov 15, 12:00 PM to 5:00 PM | Attend your assigned presentations, at most one per session. Score each presentation right after it (§3). |
| End of the last session | Scoring closes. All your scores, nominations, and your personal ranking are in. |
| After the last session | In-field judges check flagged issues for shortlisted teams. Then deliberation (§4). |
| Closing ceremony | Results are announced. |

## 1. Registration

- **When.** Before Nov 13.
- **Invitation only.** The organizers appoint the judges. Open the invitation email or the competition page, then sign up or log in with your SAIR ID.
- **Judge agreement.** Accept it on the platform. It includes the conflict-of-interest rules (§2) and the independence rule (§3.1). The review entry then appears on the website.

## 2. Problem selection

- **When.** Once the problem list is final, before Nov 13.
- **Choosing.** Choose every problem you are able to judge. You can also describe, in the optional text box, the kinds of problems you would most like to judge.
- **Balancing.** The organizers start from your choices and then balance the assignments so that every presentation has enough judges. You may be assigned a problem you did not choose.
- **Your presentations.** The organizers assign presentations after the submission deadline, Nov 15 at 10:00 AM. Each presentation has 2 to 5 judges, including at least one from the team's field when possible, and you have at most one presentation per session. Each assigned presentation shows the team's problem, report, and slides, and any supplementary materials or Lean project.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Tell the organizers about any relationship with a team that could affect impartiality.
  - **Recuse** from a team if you have a close personal or professional relationship with one of its members, such as family, an advisor or advisee, the same research group, or a close collaborator. Also recuse if you helped the team substantively during the event.
  - **Declare** if you proposed the team's problem. This does not mean automatic recusal; the organizers decide.
  - If you are assigned a team you have a conflict with, tell the organizers before the presentation.

## 3. Scoring

- **When.** Scoring opens at 12:00 PM on Nov 15, when you get an email, and closes at the end of the last session.
- **What you score.** Only the presentations assigned to you: the presentation and its Q&A. You may read the team's report, but you do not have to. After the deadline you can read every team's submission.

### 3.1 At each presentation

- **Independence.** Do not look at any LLM output until you have submitted all your scores. You do not receive LLM-suggested questions before presentations.
- **Shared problems.** You will be told which teams worked on the same problem.
- **Useful questions:**
  - definitions;
  - the key step;
  - what is new and what is already known;
  - what the AI did, and whether the team understands it;
  - how sure the team is of its claims.

### 3.2 The scoring form

Fill in one form for each presentation, on the platform:

- Correctness, Clarity, Motivation, and Interest: a whole number from 1 to 5 each, with at least one sentence of justification;
- shortlist nomination: yes or no, with a one-sentence reason if yes;
- feedback for the team;
- whether the project should continue in Round 2: yes, maybe, or no;
- any conflict of interest.

- **When to submit.** Right after the presentation, and no later than the end of the last session.
- **Drafts and edits.** You can save drafts, and you can edit your submitted scores until scoring closes. After that, a score can be changed only if the organizers reopen it.
- **Personal ranking.** By the end of the last session, also submit a personal ranking of the presentations you attended.
- **Privacy.** Your scores, justifications, and feedback are sent to the team privately. They are never published.

### 3.3 Guidelines and rubric

You score the first four dimensions. Attribution and Novelty are scored by the LLM panel only.

| Dimension | Guideline |
|---|---|
| **Correctness** | The participants should be able to discuss the mathematics relevant to their presentation in a clear, correct, and sophisticated way. They should accurately convey their uncertainty and be truthful. They should be able to define the mathematical objects under consideration. |
| **Clarity** | They should be able to form clean, correct sentences about the relevant objects, which go beyond surface level considerations. |
| **Motivation** | The participants should be able to explain what mathematical contribution they hope to make. If their project is about finding a new approach to an old result, they need to be able to explain what is unsatisfying about the old approach. If their approach is about crystalizing the connection between two related settings, then they should be able to explain why this crystallization would be useful to the relevant mathematical community. Saying that doing something is an open problem ("finding a proof of [bla] that avoids [bla] technique is an open problem") is not sufficient - they need to explain why [bla] technique is undesirable. |
| **Interest** | The participants should do something interesting during the mathathon event, related to the mathematical contribution they want to make. This can mean doing numerics, proving a lemma, or scaffolding a plan of attack. This interesting thing should demonstrate creativity and critical thinking. |
| **Attribution** (LLM panel only) | Sources are credited, and known results are not presented as new. AI use is disclosed and is consistent with the rest of the report. |
| **Novelty** (LLM panel only) | How the proof, exposition, or connection differs from known work. Failing to find a precedent does not show that something is new. |

**Notes**

- The Q&A counts toward Correctness and Clarity.
- Partial results that are honestly labeled are not penalized.
- **Interest and Novelty are different.** Novelty asks whether the work differs from what already exists. Interest asks whether the work is worthwhile and was well executed during the event.

  | Example | Interest | Novelty |
  |---|---|---|
  | A known proof re-explained through new worked examples that make a hard step transparent | High | Low |
  | A genuinely different proof that is longer and less illuminating than the known ones | Low | High |

- **None of these earns credit by itself:**
  - length;
  - using Lean (it is evidence for Correctness, and only for what it verifies);
  - using AI, or not using it;
  - saying that something is an open problem.

**Rubric.** Score each dimension as a whole number from 1 to 5. "Expected" means what a team can do in 40 hours, not a finished research paper.

| | 5 · Excellent | 4 · Good | 3 · Acceptable | 2 · Unsatisfactory | 1 · Highly unsatisfactory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *All dimensions* | *Going above and beyond what was expected* | *Few flaws and some creative elements* | *Only a few minor issues* | *A major issue or multiple minor issues* | *Many major issues* |
| **Correctness** | All claims and arguments correct and precisely stated; objects defined; the status of every claim exact; probing questions answered accurately | Correct apart from small slips; objects defined; uncertainty conveyed accurately; most technical questions answered well | Main claims correct; a few minor errors or loose definitions; uncertainty mostly clear | A significant error or unsupported claim, or several minor errors; some claims overstated | Main claims wrong or seriously overstated; key objects undefined; many errors |
| **Clarity** | Exceptionally clear and well organized; rigorous and heuristic arguments always distinguished; accessible beyond the subfield | Clear and well organized; notation defined; few rough spots | Generally easy to follow; some unclear passages or undefined notation | Hard to follow in key parts, or rigorous and heuristic arguments mixed up | Disorganized and hard to follow throughout |
| **Motivation** | Contribution sharply stated, with a compelling case for why it matters to the relevant community | Contribution clear and its importance convincingly argued, with minor gaps | Contribution stated, but the case for its importance is thin or generic | Contribution vague, or justified only by "it is an open problem" | No discernible contribution or reason |
| **Interest** | Substantial, creative event work yielding real insight, beyond what 40 hours would lead one to expect | Solid event work showing creativity and critical thinking | Reasonable event work, mostly routine | Little event work, or mostly surface-level | No meaningful work during the event |
| **Attribution** (LLM only) | Every source and form of help credited precisely; AI use fully disclosed and consistent | Sources credited, with minor omissions | Main sources credited; some peripheral gaps | A known result or argument not credited, or AI disclosure inconsistent with the report | Known work presented as the team's own |
| **Novelty** (LLM only) | A genuinely new proof, connection, or viewpoint, not found in known work | Substantial new elements on known foundations | A meaningful variation of known work | A minor variation of standard methods | Reproduces known work with no new element |

## 4. After scoring

### 4.1 LLM panel and shortlist

- **The LLM panel.** Seven models review each report: Claude Opus 5.5, GPT 6.1 sol, Gemini 4, DeepSeek 4.1 Flash, Kimi K3.1, Muse Spark (open), and Mistral Large 4. Each model reads the report without its title block and scores Correctness, Clarity, Motivation, Interest, Attribution, and Novelty. The models do not see the slides, the supplementary materials, or the presentation.
- **LLM scores are advisory.** They can add a team to the shortlist, but never remove one. An issue raised by a model counts against a team only after a human has checked it (§4.2).
- **Who is on the shortlist.** The top 10 teams by human signal, the top 10 teams by LLM signal, and every team nominated by at least one judge. All teams tied at the boundary are included.
- **Nominations** close at the end of the last session. During deliberation, any judge may still add a nomination, with a reason, until the winners are decided.

### 4.2 Triage (in-field judges)

- **What reaches triage:**
  - every issue that any model rated critical;
  - every integrity issue;
  - every team whose Attribution median is 2 or lower.
- **Your part.** You check the mathematical issues of teams in your field: for shortlisted teams before deliberation, for other teams before publication. Give each issue a status and a one-line reason:
  - **rejected:** checked, not a real problem;
  - **confirmed:** checked, a real problem.

  For shortlisted teams, also check the LLM summary against the seven raw evaluations.
- **Priority.** Critical issues come first, ordered by how many models raised them. That number sets priority only; it never proves that an issue is real.
- **Integrity issues** go to two organizers with no conflict of interest. They hear from the team before deciding anything.

### 4.3 Reading the Evidence Summary

Each shortlisted team has a one-page Evidence Summary. One is available for any other team on request. It contains:

1. **Human judges:** each judge's scores and comments, and the human signal. The human signal is each judge's mean of the four scores, averaged over the team's judges.
2. **LLM panel:**
   - the median of the models' scores for each dimension, and the medians of the proprietary and open models;
   - disagreement flags, raised when the models' highest and lowest scores differ by 2 or more;
   - the LLM signal: the mean of the medians for Correctness, Clarity, Motivation, Interest, and Novelty;
   - any missing models.
3. **LLM summary:** where the models agree, where they disagree, the merged issues, and suggested questions.
4. **Critical issues,** each with its triage status (§4.2).
5. **Lean:** the automated check results for each declaration.
6. **Notes:** missing or late materials, and any corrections.

How to read it:

- **Different artifacts.** You judged the presentation; the LLM panel judged the report. A strong difference between the two views is worth discussing, not averaging away.
- **The models are not independent.** They share much of their training data, so agreement among them is a signal, not proof.
- **Triage status.** A confirmed issue counts. A rejected issue is dropped. An unverified issue is an open question, not a finding.
- **Lean.** A declaration is verified only if the project builds, nothing it depends on uses `sorry` or a non-standard axiom, and its formal statement matches the claim in the report. A successful build alone verifies nothing, and `native_decide` does not count. Lean shows nothing about whether a proof is new or interesting.
- **Missing LLM results** never count against a team.

### 4.4 Deliberation

- **Discuss explicitly:**
  - teams where the human and LLM views differ strongly;
  - teams with disagreement flags within the LLM panel;
  - critical issues.
- **No formula.** Personal rankings are shown for reference. Human and LLM scores are never added together, and no formula selects the winners.
